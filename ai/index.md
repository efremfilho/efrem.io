AI is not magic: it is applied math, guided by human choices. Once you see that, two things happen at once — the fear shrinks and the responsibility grows.

**What this guide is for.** To give autonomy to anyone who wants to change something by building with technology. You don't need to be a tech person; you need a problem you want to solve. That takes two things: **generative AI literacy** — knowing what the machine does, where it fails, and how to ask — and **computational thinking** — knowing how to turn a problem into a flow, a step-by-step, layers. Generative AI democratized product building, and knowing how to use it [is no longer optional](/blog/en/being-ai-savvy-is-no-longer-optional/), as once happened with Word, Excel, and PowerPoint. It executes what you ask very well. What stays yours is knowing how to ask, knowing how to read what came back, and answering for the result.

**How it's organized.** There are three parts. In **Understand**, what the machine does on the inside, where bias comes from, what counts as sensitive data, and what all of this costs. In **Think**, how to break a problem down, give context, retrieve the right information, and decide between asking for an answer and asking for a script. In **Build**, how to get from a request to a product: the repository as memory, app builders, voice and images, agents on a short leash, real cases, and the responsibility of whoever ships.

**Who Lia is.** An invented character with a real problem. Lia, Bia, and Caio work at a civil society organization that does outreach: going after teenagers who have stopped going to school. In Brazil, millions of young people have not finished high school, and the reason most often given by those who left is needing to work (IBGE, 2026). On the other side, there are people going after them, and it works: outreach has already helped hundreds of thousands of children and adolescents get back to the classroom (UNICEF, 2025). Nobody there is a tech person. They heard that "AI will solve it" and decided to build: they debate the strategy, split up the work, each person implements with their own agents, and one reviews what the other did. Around them are Iara, who coordinates the partnership on the side of the city's education department, and Aída, from its technology team.

**The guide doesn't answer for her.** The journey is told as a case study, the kind where things go wrong: there's a deadline, there's a fight, and there's more than one defensible path. In each chapter there is a **scene**, which ends in a question; a **provocation**, tied to the chapter's experiment; and a **logbook**, with what was left in dispute, the paths that were on the table — each one defended by someone — and what the team did, for now. There is no answer key. Sometimes the team chooses badly, and pays for it a few chapters later. The answers depend on the school system these people work in — and yours, on your reality. What the guide offers is the way to ask and the way to check.

**How to use it.** Almost every chapter has an experiment for you to play with (look for the 🧪), ends with a **Take it to class** box — an activity and discussion questions, to use freely — and closes with the references. If you prefer paper, there's the [book version](/ai/book/), ready to print.

This material grew out of recordings of my classes at FGV EPPG, a school of public policy in Brasília, of workshops for product people, of conversations where I walk people through my process, and of what I've been writing since 2025. The text was generated with AI from that material and edited by me. It was written in Portuguese first; this is the English edition. It is a living guide: I come back, fix and expand it.

**A warning.** At several points I step outside my field: law, statistics, design, education. Where that happens, I say so and cite the people who study the subject. Found a mistake? Tell me, and I will fix it.
